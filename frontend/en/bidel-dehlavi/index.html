Meet the poet

Bidel Dehlavi

Abu’l-Ma‘ani Mirza Abdul-Qadir Bidel · 1054–1133 AH / 1644–1720 CE

A poet who used remarkably precise language to build a world out of mirrors, wonder, dust and breath, a world where every word carries its own meaning. Bidel is regarded as the greatest poet of the “Indian Style” (sabk-e hindi) and one of the most difficult and profound poets of the Persian language.

Also known as: Bedil Dehlavi, Abdul Qadir Bedil, Mirza Abdul Qadir Bedil, Abu’l-Ma‘ani Bidel.

His Life in Brief

Bidel was born in 1054 AH in Azimabad, on the banks of the Ganges, into a family of Barlas Turks. His father, Mirza Abdul-Khaliq, died before Bidel turned five, and his mother died soon after. His uncle, Mirza Qalandar, then raised him. Instead of sending him to school, Mirza Qalandar took him into the company of dervishes and people of the heart. Bidel wrote poetry from his early youth. Figures such as Shah Kabuli, Shah Maluk and, above all, Shah Qasim Huwallahi shaped his outlook.

As a young man he served for a time in the army of Prince Muhammad A‘zam, son of Aurangzeb. When he was asked to write a panegyric ode in praise of the prince, he left the post rather than compose it. He spent most of his life between Delhi, Akbarabad (Agra) and Mathura, and finally settled in Delhi. There he was supported by friends such as Nawab Shukrullah Khan.

Bidel died in Delhi in 1133 AH. His tomb was lost in the invasions and lootings of the following century. His poetry, however, lived on in Afghanistan, Central Asia and the Indian subcontinent.

Timeline

    Born in Azimabad

    In Chahar Onsor, Bidel records the year of his birth with two chronograms: “Fayz-e Quds” and “Intikhab.”

  2. c. 1059c. 1648 CE

    His father dies

    Bidel was four and a half years old. After his mother’s death, his uncle Mirza Qalandar raised him.

  3. 10681658 CE

    With the army in the war of succession

    During Shah Shuja’s revolt for the throne, the teenage Bidel travelled with the army of Mirza Abdul-Latif. The “Chandchur pond” episode told in Chahar Onsor took place on this journey.

  4. 10701660 CE

    A journey on foot to Mahsi

    A dangerous trip in the turmoil that followed Shah Shuja’s defeat. The story of the “kind old man” from this journey is one of the most memorable parts of Chahar Onsor.

  5. c. 1075c. 1664 CE

    First stay in Delhi

    The start of his bond with the city whose name he would later carry.

  6. 10781667 CE

    Muhit-e A‘zam (“The Greatest Ocean”)

    His first major masnavi, a meditation on creation and love.

  7. 10801669 CE

    Tilism-e Hayrat (“The Talisman of Wonder”)

    An allegorical masnavi about the human being and the inner faculties.

  8. 10811670 CE

    The Akbarabad dream

    A dream that Bidel narrates with great fervour at the end of Chahar Onsor.

  9. 10831672 CE

    Death of Shah Qasim Huwallahi

    One of Bidel’s most influential spiritual guides. Bidel records the chronogram of his death in Chahar Onsor.

  10. 10961685 CE

    Return to Delhi

    After two years of insecurity in Mathura, Bidel set out for Delhi with his family.

  11. 10991687 CE

    Tur-e Ma‘rifat (“The Sinai of Knowledge”)

    A masnavi describing the hills of Bairat and the experience of contemplating nature.

  12. 11001688 CE

    Seven months of illness

    A severe illness that Chahar Onsor links to the story of his portrait by the painter Anup Chitar.

  13. 11161704 CE

    Completion of Chahar Onsor

    Bidel’s autobiography; he recorded the year of its completion in a chronogram verse.

  14. 11241712 CE

    ‘Irfan (“Gnosis”)

    His longest and best-known masnavi.

  15. 11331720 CE

    Death in Delhi

    Bidel died at seventy-nine, in the last years of Mughal splendour in India.

Chahar Onsor: Bidel in His Own Words

Bidel wrote the story of his life in four parts and called each part an “element.” The book is not a simple record of events. It is an inner travelogue in which story, poetry and reflection are woven together. Some of its most memorable experiences:

  • Guides. Shah Qasim Huwallahi, Shah Kabuli and other dervishes who shaped his vision.
  • The edge of death. One night in Delhi, a morsel of sweets blocked his breath. He travelled to the “black point” of non-being and came back.
  • The master blacksmith. In Punjab, a blacksmith sharpened Bidel’s knife and refused any payment. Bidel was so overwhelmed by his grace that he “fled” the shop.
  • The portrait. A painter named Anup Chitar made a portrait of him. The portrait faded during Bidel’s illness and came back to life after he recovered.

Why Bidel Still Lives

In Afghanistan and Central Asia, Bidel has always been a beloved poet. The tradition of Bidel-khani (gatherings for reading Bidel) still continues there, and his poems were once taught in elementary schools. Both Ghalib of Delhi and Iqbal of Lahore were influenced by him.

In Iran, Bidel was little known for a long time. In the 1980s, Mohammad-Reza Shafiei-Kadkani’s book Sha‘er-e Ayeneh-ha (“The Poet of Mirrors”) and the research that followed brought him new readers.

Today Bidel is regarded as one of the classical poets closest to our own time. He speaks of wonder, awareness and the impermanence of the world with rare precision.
